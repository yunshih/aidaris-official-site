AI Can Build Anything. It Can't Tell You What to Build.

🌐 中文

September 2026 · 5 min read · Aidaris Technologies Engineering Philosophy

Engineering Judgment AI & Work Traditional Industry Digital Transformation

An auto parts distributor—three warehouses, forty thousand SKUs, thirty employees—spent three weeks rebuilding its inventory and order system. Microservices, real-time stock sync across all three warehouses, an event bus, a mobile app for the sales team, BI dashboards for the owner. Their two-person IT team did most of it with an AI coding assistant.

The problem that started the project was that a salesperson had quoted a brake assembly the warehouse no longer had, and the customer walked. The old system's stock count only updated when the warehouse keyed in receipts and shipments at the end of the day. A rule that the warehouse posts every movement within the hour, and a habit of checking stock before quoting, would have fixed it. Nobody asked.

This is the failure mode AI has made cheap and nearly invisible: building a correct solution to the wrong problem. AI makes any technical solution fast to build. It does nothing to make the right one easier to identify—and telling the difference comes not from knowing how technologies work, but from having put them into a business and paid for what went wrong.

The Brake That Disappeared

When replacing an inventory system meant a year, a vendor, and a budget the owner signed personally, that cost forced a conversation. Someone had to ask whether the system was really failing, or whether the real problem was a warehouse that posted receipts at 6 p.m. Implementation cost was a filter: only problems severe enough to justify the expense got the complex solution. Everything else got a process change or a spreadsheet—not by principle, but by budget.

That filter was never designed, so no one replaced it when it vanished. When an AI assistant can scaffold a custom inventory system in a week, there is no year-long conversation and no natural moment where someone is forced to ask "do we need this?" Breadth without a filter is not an asset. It is surface area: a sync service that fails at 8 a.m. when the counter opens, a login the warehouse staff forget, a barcode module nobody maintains. The distributors getting this right have replaced the brake deliberately—a review that asks what problem this solves before asking how, and a person, often the warehouse manager rather than IT, whose job is to say no.

Which Half AI Accelerated

There are two kinds of technical knowledge. Pattern knowledge is how multi-warehouse stock sync works, what an SKU and part-number data model looks like, how a barcode scanner talks to the accounting system. It lives in vendor documentation and in every language model's training data. It is now available in seconds.

Situational knowledge is whether this distributor, with this product catalog and this staff, will actually be better off with barcode-driven inventory—or whether it will fall apart in the first month because parts arrive from suppliers in unlabeled boxes, the same brake pad exists under five interchangeable part numbers, and the counter staff identify parts by car model and memory faster than any scanner. This knowledge is not in any documentation, because documentation describes the technology, not your warehouse. It is accumulated one way: by deploying systems, living with them, and carrying their failures forward as instinct.

AI accelerated the first kind completely and the second kind not at all. Pattern knowledge used to be a proxy for judgment—if you knew how multi-warehouse sync worked, you had probably earned it in a business that needed it. That proxy is broken. Anyone can now produce pattern-correct output with no situational judgment behind it, and it looks identical to what an experienced integrator would produce. The difference shows up at the counter, months later, as a cost no one traces back to the decision.

The Problem Statement Is Wrong

Give AI a requirement and it builds the requirement. That is the most dangerous property in the toolchain, because requirements are almost never statements of the business problem. They are someone's first guess at a solution, dressed as a need. "We need real-time inventory" means sales promised a part that wasn't there. "We need AI demand forecasting" means the senior buyer who knows which parts move before rainy season is retiring and nobody knows how he decides. "We need a mobile app" means the second-generation owner just came back from a seminar.

An engineer with situational knowledge hears the requirement and asks what happened. The lost sale is fixed by posting stock movements within the hour and checking before quoting—not an event bus. The retiring buyer is addressed by three months of documenting how he decides before anyone mentions forecasting models. AI cannot do this. It has no access to what happened last month. It takes the problem statement as given. Seeing through the statement to the actual pain is not a skill. It is scar tissue. AI has none.

Invest in Convergence, Not Breadth

The instinct when a new capability arrives is to acquire more of it. This is the wrong direction. Breadth is now free. The constraint is convergence: narrowing the enormous space of what can be built to the one thing that should be.

Convergence looks like an IT lead who stands behind the counter for a morning before opening a laptop. It looks like a review that starts from the simplest thing that would work. It is systematically under-invested in because it produces no visible output: the engineer who talks a distributor out of a platform it doesn't need saves more than the one who builds it beautifully, but only one of them has an app to show for it. Transformation budgets reward building. Nothing rewards correctly not building.

When implementation was expensive, the scarce skill was building the complex thing. Now that it is cheap, the scarce skill is knowing when not to. That knowledge is not in the model. It is in people who have put enough systems into enough businesses to know which ones they regret.
