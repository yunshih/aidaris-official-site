The Person Who Uses AI Well Is Not Who You Think

🌐 中文

November 2026 · 7 min read · Aidaris Technologies Engineering Philosophy

Orchestration AI & Work Engineering Governance Team Building Leadership

Using AI well requires neither the deepest domain expertise nor the most proficient tool skills. The two most common criteria organizations use to evaluate AI talent are both secondary.

This is not to say that expertise and tool proficiency do not matter. They do. But each carries its own risk — and those two risks, taken together, clarify what organizations actually need.

The Expert's Blind Spot

Experts using AI have a specific failure mode.

People with strong domain expertise are indeed better at identifying AI errors — within their area of familiarity. The problem is exactly that. They have strong expectations about their domain; when AI output conforms to those expectations, they accept it; when it does not, they correct it. This sounds good, but it carries a hidden assumption: that AI errors will appear where the expert is comfortable looking.

They do not always.

The most expensive AI errors tend to occur at domain boundaries: the interface between business logic and technical implementation, the gap between this organization's context and general-purpose patterns, the distance between what a customer actually needs and how the requirement was stated. In these places, the expert's expertise gives them a sense of certainty — and the AI says something that fits that certainty, even when the underlying premise has already drifted.

There is a deeper problem too: expert knowledge was built in a world where knowledge was scarce and execution was hard. The skills they are most proud of are exactly the skills AI replicates most readily. When AI can do in five minutes what took them ten years to master, they might be skeptical ("this doesn't look right") or accepting ("better than I expected") — but rarely the third and more important response: "I don't need to do this anymore. So where is my value now?" Without that question, they continue using AI to reproduce past versions of themselves, rather than using AI to reach places they couldn't go before.

The Inexperienced Person's Risk

People without much experience face an entirely different risk.

They typically have no resistance to AI. No entrenched habits to change, no "we've always done it this way" inertia. This gives them an advantage in adoption speed. The problem is that they also lack calibration.

AI output always looks fluent, confident, and complete on the surface. For someone without sufficient background knowledge, it is hard to distinguish "this answer sounds right" from "this answer is right." The work looks finished, the code runs, the document is written — but nobody can say whether it solved the right problem, or whether it will be maintainable three months from now.

This risk is harder to spot than the expert's risk, because its appearance is productivity. High throughput, fast turnaround, no obvious failures. Until a moment arrives that requires deep judgment — and that judgment capacity was never developed.

The Third Profile

Both risks point toward the same question: what does using AI well actually require?

Not deeper domain expertise. Not more proficient tool skills. What it requires is a map of the team's overall capability — knowing, within this team, whose judgment is reliable in which domain; which tasks require that judgment; which tasks can safely be handed to AI; and which handoff points need a human explicitly in charge.

This capability is called orchestration.

The person who can orchestrate is not necessarily the most technically detailed, nor the most tool-fluent. But they can do several things:

  • Break a complex problem into "parts AI can handle reliably" and "parts that require human judgment"
  • Put the right person in at the right node, rather than having everyone do everything simultaneously
  • Establish clear accountability between AI output and final outcome — not "AI did it, so nobody is responsible," but "this person is responsible for this result, even if AI did most of the work"

This is an organizational skill, not an individual one. And it is far scarcer than "being good at AI."

Organizations Are Asking the Wrong Question

Most organizations currently evaluate AI talent through an individual skill framework: Can this person use the tools? Are their prompts well-written? How much AI have they incorporated into their personal workflow?

These questions are not wrong, but they are measuring something secondary. Tool proficiency can be learned quickly; orchestration cannot. A person who is good with tools but does not know how to organize a team's judgment capacity will, at best, raise their individual output. At worst, they will replicate high-speed individual judgment errors across the entire workflow, with nobody able to identify where the problem is.

The truly scarce talent is the person who can ask these questions: Who should make this decision? Who validates the AI's output? Who is accountable for this handoff? Not because they enjoy process, but because they understand: when AI is part of the work, if accountability structure is not clear, failures accumulate quietly in the least visible places.

What organizations need is not just to find this kind of person, but to create conditions where this kind of capability can be seen and developed. Individual screening finds far less than an organizational environment can cultivate.

The bottleneck for AI effectiveness is not tools, and it is not individual talent quality. It is whether the organization understands its own capability map, and whether someone is responsible for aligning that map with how AI works. That is the question AIDARIS helps engineering organizations answer.
